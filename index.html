What is Phishing?

Phishing is a social-engineering attack that tricks people into revealing sensitive information or performing actions that compromise security. Attackers impersonate trusted entities to gain access.

Why attackers use phishing

Because it targets human trust and curiosity — often the easiest route to credentials, money, or access.

Typical attack flow

  1. Attacker
  2. Social Engineering
  3. Victim Interaction
  4. Malicious Link / Attachment
  5. Credential Theft or Malware
  6. Account / System Compromise

Recognizing Phishing Emails

Inspect the sender, subject, tone, links, and attachments carefully.

Example: Suspicious Email

From: payroll@secure-pay.example

Subject: URGENT: Your Account Will Be Suspended

Hello,

We detected unusual activity on your account. Verify now or your access will be suspended within 24 hours.

Click here to verify: https://secure-pay.example/verify

Thanks,
Example Payroll Team

  • Sender mismatch: display name and address differ
  • Urgency pressure: threats to suspend access
  • Suspicious link: unclear domain and path
  • Generic greeting: not personalized

Example: Legitimate-Looking Email (Fictional)

From: support@secure-example.com

Subject: Your Monthly Statement Is Ready

Hi Jamie,

Your statement for July is ready. Visit your account dashboard to view it. If you did not request this, contact support.

— Secure Example Team

  • Look for unexpected requests: even legitimate senders can be spoofed
  • Verify via separate channels: log in from known bookmarks, not email links

Fake Websites & URL Analysis

Inspect domains, subdomains, spelling, and paths. HTTPS alone doesn't guarantee legitimacy.

  • https://secure-example.com — looks normal
  • https://secure-example-login.com — lookalike domain
  • https://login.secure-example.com.example.org — subdomain trick
  • https://exarnple-security.com — typosquatting

Warning signs: unexpected redirects, shortened links, domains that don't match official sites, and mismatched SSL certificate names.

Social Engineering Tactics

Attackers manipulate emotions — urgency, fear, authority, curiosity, and greed — to make victims act without verifying.

Urgency

Scenario: "Immediate action required" to avoid penalty. Warning: short deadlines, pressure. Defensive response: pause, verify via known contact.

Authority

Scenario: "Your manager asked..." Warning: impersonation. Defensive response: confirm by calling the manager or sending a separate message.

Curiosity & Greed

Scenario: "You've won a prize" or enticing links. Warning: unsolicited offers. Defensive response: don't click; verify with sender.

Common Phishing Types

  • Email Phishing: broad-scope fraudulent messages.
  • Spear Phishing: targeted to a specific person.
  • Whaling: targeted at executives.
  • Smishing: SMS-based phishing.
  • Vishing: voice call scams.
  • BEC: Business Email Compromise targeting finance.
  • Clone Phishing: duplicate of a legitimate email with malicious changes.
  • QR-code Phishing: malicious QR codes leading to fake sites.

Real-World Case Studies (Educational)

Incident: Target data breach (2013)

What happened: Attackers gained access to Target's network via credentials stolen from a third-party HVAC vendor, leading to the theft of millions of customer payment card records.

Attack technique: Credential compromise from a trusted vendor and lateral movement into point-of-sale systems.

Warning signs: Unusual vendor access, unexpected authentication activity, and systems behaving abnormally.

Defensive lesson: Enforce least-privilege access for third parties, monitor vendor activity, segment networks, and require multi-factor authentication (MFA).

Incident: 2016 Democratic spear-phishing campaign (publicized 2016)

What happened: Targeted spear-phishing messages led to credential compromise of several high-profile email accounts, resulting in data exposure.

Attack technique: Highly targeted phishing emails crafted to appear relevant to recipients (spear-phishing), including fake login prompts and password-reset lures.

Warning signs: Unexpected password-reset notices, messages tailored to personal or organization context, and unfamiliar sender addresses despite realistic display names.

Defensive lesson: Use strong MFA, verify password-reset requests via separate channels, and provide targeted phishing-awareness training to high-risk roles.

Incident: FACC Business Email Compromise (2016)

What happened: An Austrian aerospace supplier (FACC) reported fraudulent wire transfers after attackers impersonated executives and instructed finance staff to transfer funds.

Attack technique: Business Email Compromise (BEC) — email impersonation and social-engineering to authorize fraudulent payments.

Warning signs: Unusual payment requests, pressure to act urgently, requests for secrecy, and changes in payment details without standard validation.

Defensive lesson: Enforce verification procedures for payment changes (voice verification using known numbers), segregation of duties, and transaction approval workflows.
